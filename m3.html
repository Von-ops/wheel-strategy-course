Module 3: Cash-secured puts

Diagnose first. Before reading: if a $50 put is assigned, how much cash do you pay, and would you still want the shares after a sharp drop?

The mechanics

You sell a put and set aside cash equal to the strike × 100 for each contract. If the stock stays above the strike, the put expires and you keep the premium. If it falls below, you buy 100 shares at the strike. Your effective price is the strike minus the premium.

Pick the company first

Choose a steady, liquid stock you would happily own for months, decide what price you would pay, and only then look at the option. Use 0.20–0.30 delta (put deltas show as negative) and 21–45 days. Delta is only a rough guide to the odds of assignment.

Size by worst case, not by premium

The most you can lose is the strike minus the premium, times 100, if the stock goes to zero. That number, not the premium, has to fit your max-loss rule. Having enough cash is one test; fitting the risk limit is a separate one.

Keep collateral separate

The cash backing a put is spoken for. Do not count it toward your cash floor or use it for anything else while the put is open.

Worked example

Illustrative: XYZ trades at $52. You sell one $50 put, 30 days out, for $1.00, collecting $100 and setting aside $5,000. Breakeven is $49. Return on the collateral is 2% for the month (about 24% annualized, not a forecast). If XYZ falls to $42, you buy at $50 and are down $700 after the premium.

Teach it back

Describe the promise you sold, the cash you reserved, the worst-case dollar loss, and what owning the shares would add to your portfolio.

Applied scenario

The stock you sold a put on falls 20% the week before expiration. Do you still want to own it at your strike? Write your plan before you check the price again.

Quick quiz

You collect $100 on a $50 put. What is the most you can lose?
The put fits your available cash. Does it automatically fit your rules?
